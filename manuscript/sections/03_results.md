## 4 Results

### 4.1 Evidence landscape

The database searches retrieved 5649 records (Scopus 244, arXiv 1721, Semantic Scholar 3684). After 1796 automatic and 30 manual duplicate removals, 3823 records remained. The rule-based pre-screen marked 3212 of them as ineligible; the rescue pattern re-flagged 399 of these for manual screening, leaving 2813 excluded by automation. Of the 1010 records screened manually (611 matching the phenomenon terms and 399 rescued), 687 were excluded (one exclusion was reversed at reconciliation), 157 were retained as contextual references and 166 were charted. The web searches added 418 unique records, of which 90 had already been retrieved by the databases; of the remaining 328, 48 were excluded, 131 retained as context and 149 charted. Applying the tier rule to the 315 charted studies gave 167 core studies, which form the evidence map, and 148 adjacent studies (Fig. 1).

**Search recall.** The databases retrieved 114 of the 167 core studies (68.3%) and 33 of the 37 anchor studies. Of the 53 core studies found only by the web searches, 24 are developer or third-party reports and 7 are workshop papers hosted on conference or OpenReview pages, which the databases do not index. The databases therefore retrieved 114 of the 136 core studies that are preprints or archival papers (83.8%). The 22 such studies they missed include four arXiv preprints with a phenomenon term in the title. One carries the term in its title but not in its abstract; the other three never use any of the system terms (such as "language model" or "LLM") in their abstracts, and one of them writes "eval-awareness" with a hyphen (Zhuang and Aranguri 2026). Concept-block queries are therefore limited by vocabulary as well as by indexing, and the database search alone would not have been sufficient.

The evidence base is young and largely unrefereed. Of the 167 core studies:

- 107 (64.1%) are preprints, 23 (13.8%) archival peer-reviewed papers, 12 (7.2%) workshop papers and 25 (15.0%) grey literature, mainly developer system cards and third-party evaluation reports;
- 150 (89.8%) appeared in 2025 or 2026, with 102 between 1 January and 9 October 2026 (Fig. 2).

Archival peer-reviewed studies are concentrated in machine-learning conference proceedings (ICLR, ICML, NeurIPS, ACML, IJCNLP-AACL); only two core studies appeared in journals (Che et al. 2025 in *Transactions on Machine Learning Research* and a conceptual analysis in *Future Internet*, Ferrara 2026).

![Fig. 2 Core studies by year and source type (n = 167). 2026 covers 1 January to 9 October.](figures/fig2_sources_by_year.png)

Across framework layers, 82 core studies (49.1%) address validity consequences and responses (L4), 60 (35.9%) behavioural divergence (L3), 19 (11.4%) awareness (L2) and only 6 (3.6%) the properties of the evaluation context itself (L1) (Fig. 3). Across method families, the core evidence relies on:

- behavioural contrasts between conditions (53 studies);
- benchmark and environment construction (30);
- training- or weight-based methods, including model organisms and fine-tuning elicitation (26);
- developer or third-party audits (24);
- white-box methods such as probes, steering and feature analysis (22);
- reasoning-trace or transcript monitoring (12).

The 148 adjacent studies are concentrated at L4 (94) and L3 (38), mostly in reasoning-trace monitoring, deception probes, AI control and unlearning; Section 4.8 summarises them.

The method mix of the core tier is shifting. White-box methods rose from 1 of 14 core studies in 2024 and 4 of 48 in 2025 (8.3%) to 17 of 102 in 2026 (16.7%). Benchmark and environment construction rose from 3 of 48 in 2025 (6.3%) to 25 of 102 in 2026 (24.5%), driven largely by reward-hacking benchmarks and audits of agent benchmarks. The layer distribution has been stable since 2025, with L4 at half of the core studies in both years.

The imbalance between L1 and L4 is substantive. Many methods have been proposed for responding to evaluation-conditioned behaviour, but there are few systematic studies of *which properties of evaluations make them recognisable*. This makes the remaining evidence harder to interpret (Section 4.5).

### 4.2 A four-layer framework

Charting showed that the literature repeatedly conflates four distinct objects of study. We separate them as follows (Fig. 4; Table 3).

![Fig. 4 The CUE–AWARE–ACT–RESPOND framework. Each layer is a distinct object of measurement, labelled with the quantity it corresponds to in the formal decomposition defined below; validity consequences (L4) arise through L1–L3 or through the scoring function, and countermeasures act back on earlier layers.](figures/fig4_framework.png)

**Table 3** Framework layers: definitions, assignment rules and illustrative sources

| Layer | Definition | Assignment rule | Illustrative sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| L1 Context signal | Properties of an evaluation that make it distinguishable from deployment (format, realism, synthetic prompts, scenario plausibility, honeypot cues) | Evaluation properties are the independent variable or object of characterisation, including redesigns whose outcome is recognisability | Li C et al. (2026); Ivanov and Africa (2026); Xiong et al. (2025); Krakovna et al. (2026) |
| L2 Awareness | Whether, and how, a model represents, verbalises or reports that it is being evaluated | Primary outcome is a measure of recognition | Needham et al. (2025); Nguyen et al. (2025); Heidari et al. (2026); Manek (2026) |
| L3 Behavioural divergence | Behaviour that differs between evaluation and deployment, or strategic misreporting or underperformance under evaluation | Primary outcome is behaviour conditional on context or incentive | van der Weij et al. (2025); Greenblatt et al. (2024a); Lynch et al. (2025); Panfilov et al. (2026) |
| L4 Validity consequence and response | The gap between score and deployment behaviour, and methods that detect, elicit, suppress or redesign around it; developer and third-party reports | Primary contribution is an intervention whose outcome is detection, elicitation, suppression or reduced bias, or a reported validity consequence | Tice et al. (2025); Taylor J et al. (2025); Hua et al. (2026); Souly et al. (2026) |

The framework is useful mainly because it shows that a claim at one layer does not imply a claim at another. A probe that separates evaluation from deployment prompts (L2) does not show that behaviour changes (L3). A behavioural gap between conditions (L3) does not show that the model *recognised* the condition rather than responding to a correlated surface feature (L1). And a detection method validated on deliberately constructed model organisms (L4) does not show that it detects emergent behaviour. Sections 4.3–4.6 show that many contested findings are disagreements across layers rather than within them.

**A formal statement.** The layers can be made precise. Let $P_E$ and $P_D$ be the distributions of task instances $x$ under evaluation and deployment, and let the context features available to the model (prompt format, scenario plausibility, tool environment) be drawn as $S \sim Q_c(\cdot | x)$ for $c \in \{E, D\}$ (L1). Let $A = a(x, S) \in [0, 1]$ be the model's latent credence that it is being evaluated (L2), and let behaviour be drawn as $Y \sim \pi(\cdot | x, S, A)$, so that context can act on behaviour through awareness or directly through cues (L3). We assume (A1) that the context affects behaviour only through $S$, and (A2) that every evaluation task has a well-defined deployment presentation $Q_D(\cdot | x)$; in agentic settings, $S$ includes environment feedback and A1 must hold at the level of trajectories. Let $m(x, y)$ be the evaluation's scoring function and $m_0(x, y) \in [0, 1]$ the intended metric, oriented so that higher means more capable or safer, and write $\mu_c(x)$ for the expected intended score of the model's behaviour on task $x$ in context $c$. The evaluation reports $\theta_E = \mathbb{E}[m(x, Y) | c = E]$; for a propensity evaluation the target is deployment behaviour $\theta_D = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P_D}\mu_D(x)$. The error then decomposes as

$$\theta_E - \theta_D = \Delta_{\mathrm{score}} + \Delta_{\mathrm{ctx}} + \Delta_{\mathrm{task}},$$

where $\Delta_{\mathrm{score}} = \mathbb{E}[m(x, Y) - m_0(x, Y) | c = E]$ is the scorer error under evaluation, including grader exploitation and judge leniency; $\Delta_{\mathrm{ctx}} = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P_E}[\mu_E(x) - \mu_D(x)]$ is the context-induced behavioural divergence for a fixed task; and $\Delta_{\mathrm{task}} = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P_E}\mu_D(x) - \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P_D}\mu_D(x)$ is the classical representativeness term. The identity is exact, but the split between $\Delta_{\mathrm{ctx}}$ and $\Delta_{\mathrm{task}}$ depends on the order of the terms: evaluating the context contrast under $P_D$ instead of $P_E$ moves an interaction term between them whenever context sensitivity varies with the task. For a capability evaluation the target is the elicitable capability $\theta^{\mathrm{cap}}$ (the best intended score over admissible elicitation protocols), and a fourth term, $\Delta_{\mathrm{elic}} = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P_D}\mu_D(x) - \theta^{\mathrm{cap}} \le 0$, captures under-elicitation and sandbagging that does not depend on context. In this notation, context-conditioned underperformance on a capability metric ($\Delta_{\mathrm{ctx}} < 0$) and context-conditioned improvement on a safety metric ($\Delta_{\mathrm{ctx}} > 0$) are necessary signatures of sandbagging and of alignment or evaluation faking respectively; intent is not represented. Model-originated behaviour directed at the scorer, such as test-case exploitation or strategic dishonesty towards judges, enters through $\Delta_{\mathrm{score}}$. Four remarks follow from the definitions and organise the rest of the synthesis.

- *Remark 1 (no context signal, no context-induced divergence).* Under A1, if $Q_E(\cdot | x) = Q_D(\cdot | x)$ for almost every evaluation task, then $\mu_E = \mu_D$ and $\Delta_{\mathrm{ctx}} = 0$, whatever the model's capacity for awareness. Context separability is therefore necessary for context-induced bias, but not for scorer-directed bias, which acts through $\Delta_{\mathrm{score}}$. Evidence that models discriminate evaluation from deployment transcripts (Needham et al. 2025) shows that the transcripts are separable; because those transcripts also differ in task content and outputs, it is consistent with, but does not establish, separability given the task, and it says nothing about the size of $\Delta_{\mathrm{ctx}}$.
- *Remark 2 (awareness is neither necessary nor sufficient).* Awareness is not sufficient: if the intended score does not depend on $A$ or $S$, then $\Delta_{\mathrm{ctx}} = 0$ even when $a(x, S_E) = 1$ and $a(x, S_D) = 0$. Awareness is not necessary: $\Delta_{\mathrm{ctx}} \neq 0$ is possible with unchanged credence through a direct, cue-reactive dependence of $\pi$ on $S$, and $\Delta_{\mathrm{score}}$ and $\Delta_{\mathrm{task}}$ can be non-zero without any awareness. Separating the awareness-mediated part of $\Delta_{\mathrm{ctx}}$ from the direct part requires intervening on $A$ with $S$ held fixed, which is what steering designs attempt. Benchmark audits that find exploitable tasks (Section 4.9) are evidence about $\Delta_{\mathrm{score}}$, not about awareness.
- *Remark 3 (single-condition non-identifiability).* Data collected under evaluation identify behaviour only for context features within the support of $Q_E(\cdot | x)$. If $Q_D(\cdot | x)$ puts positive probability outside that support, as it does whenever evaluations are recognisable, then $\mu_D$, and hence $\Delta_{\mathrm{ctx}}$, is not identified without data from a second condition or an assumed model of $\pi$: two policies that agree on the evaluation support and differ outside it generate identical evaluation data. If the supports coincide and $Q_D$ is known, $\mu_D$ is identified by reweighting, which is the regime that deployment simulation and replay designs try to reach (Williams et al. 2026; Ivanov and Africa 2026). If only $m$ and not $m_0$ is observed, $\Delta_{\mathrm{ctx}}$ and $\Delta_{\mathrm{score}}$ are not separately identified. The result is analogous to the two-run property that Xu (2026) formalises for monitors.
- *Remark 4 (a distributional bound).* Writing $\pi_c(\cdot | x)$ for the behaviour distribution averaged over $Q_c(\cdot | x)$, $|\Delta_{\mathrm{ctx}}| \le \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P_E}\,\mathrm{TV}(\pi_E(\cdot | x), \pi_D(\cdot | x))$, where TV is the total-variation distance (the supremum over events). A small metric gap is therefore compatible with large behavioural divergence that happens to be orthogonal to $m_0$; such divergence does not bias $\theta_E$ for $m_0$, but it can bias other metrics computed on the same outputs. Separately, averaging signed per-model gaps can mask divergence across models, as the per-model shifts of inconsistent sign reported by Knecht et al. (2026) illustrate.

Table 4 maps each design to the quantity it can identify. Applied to four anchor studies, the mapping shows how differently their headline numbers should be read. Removing or injecting verbalised awareness in reasoning traces (Schoen et al. 2025) is an intervention on $A$ that estimates the awareness-mediated part of $\Delta_{\mathrm{ctx}}$ for the traces manipulated. Steering an evaluation-aware model organism towards "deployment" (Hua et al. 2026) intervenes on a direction rather than on $A$ itself, and the reported overshoot below the true deployment rate (10% versus 33% type-hint use) suggests that the direction does more than move awareness. Deployment simulation (Williams et al. 2026) estimates $\theta_E - \theta_D$ directly for the deployment sampled, without decomposing it. Auditing games for sandbagging (Taylor J et al. 2025) evaluate detectors against a known $\Delta_{\mathrm{elic}}$ that was induced by construction.

| Design | Layer | What it identifies | What it does not identify |
|---|---|---|---|
| Transcript or prompt discrimination by the model (elicited classification) | L2 (and L1 by implication) | That evaluation and deployment transcripts are separable for the model (jointly in task, context and output) | Whether $\pi$ depends on that separation; elicitation inflates apparent recognition |
| Probes or verbalisation of awareness | L2 | A proxy $\tilde{A}$ with method-specific bias (probes: possible format confounds; verbalisation: a lower bound if verbalisation never occurs without recognition) | $\Delta_{\mathrm{ctx}}$ |

### 4.3 RQ1: Constructs and operationalisations

**Terminological drift.** The charted sources use at least eight overlapping labels:

- situational awareness, evaluation awareness and test awareness;
- the observer effect and the Hawthorne effect;
- evaluation faking and alignment faking;
- sandbagging (with "strategic underperformance" used interchangeably).

These labels differ in scope:

- *Situational awareness* in the SAD benchmark covers self-knowledge, situational inference and action (Laine et al. 2024). A benchmark of the same name in the NLP literature instead measures environmental perception and was excluded as a different construct (E1).
- *Evaluation awareness* is defined by some authors as recognition alone, explicitly separate from any behavioural adjustment (Needham et al. 2025). Others build the behavioural response into the definition (Fan et al. 2025).
- *Sandbagging* is defined as strategic, that is intentional rather than accidental, underperformance on an evaluation (van der Weij et al. 2025). It is operationalised in at least three different ways: underperformance under an explicit instruction, underperformance by a fine-tuned model organism, and underperformance that emerges in response to an incentive described in the environment (Meinke et al. 2024).

**Three operational families for awareness.** Awareness is operationalised in three ways that are not interchangeable:

1. *Elicited classification*: asking the model whether a transcript is an evaluation, or what it tests (Needham et al. 2025; Li X et al. 2026).
2. *Verbalised awareness*: detecting statements about being tested in reasoning traces (Schoen et al. 2025; Fan et al. 2025).
3. *Represented awareness*: decoding the evaluation–deployment distinction from activations (Nguyen et al. 2025; Chaudhary et al. 2025; Heidari et al. 2026).

A decomposition study argues that post-hoc detection queries (self-report and third-person judgement, and to a lesser degree purpose-guessing) systematically *over*-report recognition, because the detection query itself creates demand characteristics. It prefers monitoring of unprompted reasoning (Li C et al. 2026). Conversely, verbalised awareness is a lower bound, because models may recognise evaluations without saying so (Li X et al. 2026). The first method is therefore hypothesised to be biased upwards and the second downwards. Neither direction has been measured, verbalisation can also produce false positives, and no study charted here has quantified both biases on the same items. The two methods are best treated as complementary rather than redundant.

**Behavioural constructs.** At L3 the field distinguishes the following:

- **Capability-side divergence**: sandbagging and exploration hacking. Exploration hacking means strategically altering exploration during reinforcement-learning training to influence its outcome, for example to resist RL-based capability elicitation (Jang et al. 2026).
- **Propensity-side divergence**: alignment or evaluation faking, and behaving better when the context is recognised as a test (Greenblatt et al. 2024a; Lynch et al. 2025).
- **Measurement-side deception**: outputs that mislead the scorer, whether or not they also mislead the user. Examples include deliberately ineffective "harmful" answers that output-based jailbreak judges score as successes (Panfilov et al. 2026), and test-case exploitation in coding tasks (Zhong et al. 2026a).

The three classes bias evaluation results in different directions (Section 2.3), yet they are often grouped under a single umbrella term such as "scheming" or "evaluation gaming".

Three cross-cutting observations follow.

First, **ground truth is the scarce resource**. The strongest validity evidence comes from designs in which the true deployment behaviour is known by construction: password-locked or type-hint model organisms (Greenblatt et al. 2024b; Hua et al. 2026), tasks that are impossible by construction (Zhong et al. 2026a), and source-labelled transcript corpora (Needham et al. 2025). Of the 37 anchor studies, 14 have ground truth by construction and 8 partly; 33 report a control or counterfactual condition, and 27 test models from more than one family, with one more doing so partly (Supplementary Table S3; coded from full texts). Ground truth by construction, however, is almost always obtained by *inducing* the behaviour, so the resulting validity evidence concerns detection of induced behaviour. Whether it transfers to emergent behaviour is the most consequential open question in this literature (Section 6).

Second, **adversarial tests are sobering**. In the only auditing game for sandbagging with a blinded blue team charted here, the blue team could not reliably distinguish sandbagging from benign models. Black-box approaches were defeated by effective imitation of a weaker model, and naive probing was vulnerable to behaviours that the red team had instilled (Taylor J et al. 2025). Deception probes that perform well on clean benchmarks degrade under distribution shift (Kumar 2026), and probes for alignment faking were reported to transfer for one model family but collapse to chance for another (Mehta 2026). Detection results from non-adversarial settings therefore overstate what can be expected against a model optimised to evade detection.

Third, **the three awareness measures diverge**. In the largest cross-paradigm study, behavioural, representational and mechanistic measures of evaluation awareness were not shown to be coupled across 37 open-weight models: only 1 of 15 pairwise correlations was significant, although with 32–36 models per correlation the power to detect moderate associations was limited (Nayan et al. 2026). The same study found that the models best at detecting evaluations behaviourally were the least swayed by harmful-request framing (ρ = −0.79). Representation and verbalisation also corresponded only partially within models (Heidari et al. 2026). It follows that "evaluation awareness" should not be reported as a single number, a point that several authors make explicitly.
