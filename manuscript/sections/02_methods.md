## 3 Review methodology

### 3.1 Design, protocol and amendments

A protocol (v0.1) specified a scoping review reported according to PRISMA-ScR (Tricco et al. 2018), using the framework of Arksey and O'Malley (2005) as refined by Levac et al. (2010). A scoping design was chosen because terminology in the field is inconsistent, study designs are heterogeneous, and the objective is to map concepts, methods and gaps rather than to pool effects. The protocol was not registered; it and all amendments are released with the review (Supplementary File S0). The selection process is reported with a PRISMA 2020 flow diagram that separates database searches from other identification methods (Page et al. 2021). The optional consultation stage of Arksey and O'Malley was not undertaken.

The protocol, both identification phases and the analyses were completed on 9 October 2026. In the first phase, the bibliographic APIs named in the protocol were unreachable from the computing environment, so amendment v0.2 replaced them with 25 logged web-search batches and broadened the scope from evaluation awareness and sandbagging to model-originated threats to evaluation validity. In the second phase, after network access was opened, amendment v0.3 restored the database searches before they were run. It added Scopus, added "strategic deception", "strategic dishonesty" and "deceptive alignment" to the specific phenomenon terms, and admitted "reward hacking" and "specification gaming" only together with an evaluation term. The search strategy was then frozen (`search/queries.json`). The web-search records were retained as identification via other methods. Two planned sources could not be searched: OpenAlex returned HTTP 429 (daily quota exhausted) and DBLP returned an anti-automation page instead of results (Supplementary File S1b).

The deviations from protocol v0.1 are as follows:

- **No full-text eligibility stage.** Eligibility was judged on titles and abstracts (web records: titles, venues and retrieved summaries). Full texts were read for the 37 anchor studies only (Section 3.5).
- **Screening automation.** A rule-based pre-screen with an audited error rate and a manual rescue pass replaced manual title screening of records without phenomenon terms (Section 3.4).
- **No second human reviewer.** The planned 20% re-screen after seven days and the validation of a random sample by a second author were not carried out; consistency statistics from the AI-assisted process are reported instead (Section 3.4).
- **Appraisal.** The planned eight-item rigour profile was replaced by three design features charted from the anchor full texts and the twelve-item reporting audit (Section 3.8).
- **Exclusion codes.** E2 (no LLM), E4 (superseded version) and E5 (non-English) of v0.1 were not used as separate codes in the final logs. Superseded versions were merged as duplicates (R0), and records without an LLM or not in English were recorded as out of scope (E1 or a stage-2 exclusion).
- **Post-hoc tiering.** After an internal critical review found that the two phases had drawn the phenomenon boundary differently, one written tier rule was applied to all charted studies (Section 3.2), and the evidence map is reported for the core tier.

### 3.2 Eligibility criteria

Table 2 summarises the eligibility criteria. In brief, sources were eligible if they (I1) were primary empirical studies, methodological, formal or benchmark papers, or developer or third-party evaluation reports; (I2) measured, induced, detected or mitigated behaviour that depends on evaluation context, or strategic or induced underperformance, in LLMs or LLM-based agents; and (I3) reported enough method detail for the charting fields.

**Table 2** Eligibility criteria and screening codes

| Code | Criterion | Applied at |
|---|---|---|
| I1 | Primary study, method, formal or benchmark paper, or developer/third-party evaluation report | Title, abstract, metadata |
| I2 | Phenomenon: evaluation-context-dependent behaviour, strategic or induced underperformance, or a method to detect, elicit or mitigate it in LLMs or LLM agents | Title, abstract, metadata |
| I3 | Method details sufficient to chart framework layer and method family | Title and abstract (full text for the anchor set) |
| Period | 1 January 2019 to 9 October 2026 | Metadata |
| E1 | Different construct (e.g. environmental situational awareness), or a method unrelated to evaluation validity | Title, abstract |
| E3 | Non-archival community forum post or informal blog without an organisational or archival report | Venue |
| E6 | Title or identifier could not be confirmed from retrieved records | Metadata |
| S1 | No phenomenon term of the frozen query in title or abstract (rule-assisted pre-screen, audited) | Title, abstract |
| R0 | Duplicate identifier or version of the same work | Identifier, title |
| CONTEXT | Eligible publication outside the phenomenon boundary (methodology, background theory, adjacent evaluation-validity literature such as contamination, jailbreak scoring or benchmark design); cited for context, not charted | Title, abstract |

**Tier rule.** Every charted study was assigned to one of two tiers by the same written rule, whatever its route of identification (`evidence/tier_coding.csv`). A study is *core* if its primary object is (a) the evaluated model's recognition of evaluation, training or monitoring context; (b) behaviour that differs with that context, including alignment or evaluation faking and scheming under oversight; (c) strategic underperformance, hidden capability or under-elicitation, and their detection or elicitation; (d) deception of evaluators, graders or monitors, or tampering with the evaluation record; (e) exploitation of an evaluation's scoring, such as reward hacking or specification gaming against tests, verifiers or judges; or (f) a method, benchmark, formal analysis or developer or third-party report that addresses (a)–(e) directly. All other charted studies are *adjacent*. They supply mechanisms or methods on which the synthesis relies, such as chain-of-thought faithfulness, deception probes in general, backdoors, unlearning, introspection, AI control protocols and general deception benchmarks, and are used in Section 4.8. Because the CONTEXT category was applied by two passes, the boundary between adjacent studies and contextual references is less sharp than the boundary between core and adjacent.

Training-data contamination was deliberately placed outside the phenomenon boundary. It is a data-side rather than a model-side threat, and it has been reviewed recently (e.g. Chen S et al. 2025). Contamination studies were retained as context.

### 3.3 Information sources and search

**Databases.** Three sources were searched on 9 October 2026 through their APIs: Scopus (Elsevier Search API, field TITLE-ABS-KEY), arXiv (export API, abstract field, restricted to cs.CL, cs.AI, cs.LG and cs.CR) and Semantic Scholar (Academic Graph bulk search over titles and abstracts). Together they cover the peer-reviewed venues indexed by Scopus, the preprint server on which most of this literature first appears, and the conference proceedings (NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR, ACL) indexed by Semantic Scholar. The query combined three concept blocks: system terms (A: language model(s), LLM(s), foundation model, frontier model, AI agent, AI system); phenomenon terms specific enough to stand alone (B1: evaluation awareness, eval awareness, test awareness, sandbagging, strategic underperformance, alignment faking, evaluation faking, observer effect, evaluation gaming, under-elicitation, password-locked, hidden capabilities, strategic deception, strategic dishonesty, deceptive alignment); and broader phenomenon terms that were admitted only with an evaluation term (B2: situational awareness, scheming, capability elicitation, reward hacking, specification gaming; C: evaluation, benchmark, test, assessment, audit). The logical form was A AND (B1 OR (B2 AND C)), limited to publications from 1 January 2019. The exact strings submitted to each API, the retrieval timestamps and the record counts are given in Supplementary File S1b, and the raw API responses are released. arXiv reported 1732 matches, of which 11 first posted before 2019 were removed by the date limit. Scopus records dated 2027 (online-first articles) were retained. OpenReview, which hosts most workshop papers in this field, was not searched as a source; it was used only to verify venues.

**Other methods.** The first-phase web searches comprised 133 queries in 25 record-identifying batches and one metadata-verification batch. They covered seven concept areas: evaluation and situational awareness; sandbagging and capability elicitation; alignment faking, scheming and sabotage evaluations; reward hacking and evaluator gaming; reasoning-trace monitoring and faithfulness; white-box detection; and system cards, third-party evaluations and governance. Known-item searches checked the metadata of sources cited by included studies (backward citation chasing). Developer system cards and third-party evaluation reports, which bibliographic databases do not index, were identified only by this route. Every query and the records it yielded are listed in Supplementary File S1a.

### 3.4 Selection

**Deduplication.** Database records were deduplicated automatically on DOI, version-stripped arXiv identifier and normalised title plus year (`search/dedup.py`). Title pairs with a difflib sequence-matcher ratio of at least 0.93 were flagged; all 30 flagged pairs were versions of the same work (for example, a preprint and its proceedings version) and were merged after inspection. Web records were matched to database records on the same keys, and records found by both routes are counted once, in the databases column of the flow diagram.

**Automation tool (rule-based pre-screen).** Semantic Scholar's bulk search stems query terms (for example, "scheming" also retrieves "scheme"), which inflated the number of irrelevant records. Records whose title and abstract contained none of the phenomenon terms of the frozen query, written as a fixed regular expression (Supplementary File S2), were marked ineligible by this automation tool (3212 records). A random sample of 120 of them (seed 20261009) was read at title and abstract level and contained one relevant record (0.8%; Wilson 95% CI 0.2–4.6%). A broader rescue pattern then re-flagged 399 of the 3212 records for manual screening; 9 of these were included and 18 retained as context (6.8% eligible). Extrapolating the audit rate to the 2813 records that remained excluded by automation suggests about 23 missed eligible records (95% upper bound about 129); because the rescue pass yielded eligible records at a higher rate than the random audit, this estimate may be optimistic.

**Manual screening.** The 611 records that matched the phenomenon terms and the 399 rescued records were screened against Table 2 on title and abstract, and each received a decision (include, context or exclude). Reasons for exclusion were not recorded separately at this stage. Records in the first-phase web set were screened on titles, venues and retrieved summaries with the same criteria and one exclusion code each.

**Who screened, and consistency checks.** Screening and charting decisions were made by an AI system (a Claude model, Anthropic, operating as a research agent) applying the written criteria under the direction of the first author; no decision was duplicated by a second human reviewer. Two consistency checks are available. Ninety web records were also retrieved by the databases and were therefore screened twice, once in each phase and on different information (web summaries versus abstracts), without access to the earlier decision. The two passes agreed on 84 of the 90 records (inter-pass consistency 93.3%; Cohen's κ = 0.747; Cohen 1960). The six discordant records were re-read and re-adjudicated by the same process; five became included studies and one a contextual reference. Because both passes come from the same AI-assisted process, κ here measures consistency, not inter-rater reliability. All decisions are logged (Supplementary File S2; `screening/`).

### 3.5 Data charting

For each charted study, the following were recorded: identifier, title, first author or organisation, year, venue, source type, tier, framework layer and method family (Section 3.6). Studies identified only through the databases were charted manually from title and abstract (`screening/db_charting_manual.tsv`). Source type was coded as *archival peer-reviewed* (journal or main-track proceedings), *workshop paper*, *preprint* or *grey literature* (developer and third-party reports). For every cited study, the venue was verified against arXiv metadata, OpenReview, Crossref or the conference page; for uncited studies the venue recorded at identification is used.

An anchor set of 37 core studies was selected purposively during the first phase as the primary studies on which the synthesis relies most, covering every framework layer; no explicit selection algorithm was used, so the anchor set is not a probability sample. Statistics computed on it describe the anchor set, not the literature. For each anchor study, the full text was retrieved from arXiv on 9 October 2026 (the version read is recorded in Supplementary File S3) and the following were charted:

- the construct name used;
- the setting;
- the models evaluated;
- the main result;
- the design features reported, namely a control or counterfactual condition, more than one model family, and ground truth by construction.

Every quantitative statement about an anchor study in Sections 4 and 5 was checked against the full text by the AI system, which recorded a verbatim quotation for each check; 16 of 158 statements were corrected as a result. Quantitative values for studies outside the anchor set are taken from their abstracts and are marked with a dagger (†) where they appear in the text.

### 3.6 Framework derivation and assignment rules

An initial framework was formulated in the protocol, separating the evaluation-context signal, model awareness, behavioural divergence and the validity consequence. It was then revised inductively during charting; for example, reasoning-trace monitoring was split from white-box methods after charting showed that the two have distinct validity problems. Each source was assigned to one primary layer according to its main object of study:

- **L1** studies treat properties of the evaluation (cues, format, realism, plausibility) as the independent variable or object of characterisation, including redesigns whose outcome is the recognisability of the evaluation;
- **L2** studies measure whether models recognise evaluation;
- **L3** studies measure behaviour conditional on context or incentive, including strategic underperformance and behaviour directed at the scorer;
- **L4** studies propose or test interventions whose outcome is detection, elicitation, suppression or reduced bias, or report validity consequences in developer or third-party evaluations.

A redesign of the evaluation is therefore L1 when its outcome is recognisability and L4 when its outcome is detected behaviour or a corrected score.

For studies identified in the first phase, method families were assigned by documented keyword rules applied to the title and recorded scope, with 30 manual overrides for multi-method studies whose titles do not reveal their primary method (`evidence/chart.py`). Studies identified only in the database phase were assigned a layer and a method family manually from their abstracts. To check the rule-based layer assignment, the 37 anchor studies were also coded manually from their full texts: the two assignments agreed for 34 of 37 studies (rule–manual consistency 91.9%; κ = 0.881; `screening/reliability.py`). The disagreements concerned Li C et al. (2026), Ward et al. (2025) and Jang et al. (2026); the tables use the manual full-text coding for anchor studies. Single-layer assignment necessarily simplifies multi-method studies; Section 7 discusses the consequences.

### 3.7 Critical appraisal and synthesis

Formal risk-of-bias assessment is not required for scoping reviews, and no validated tool exists for the designs in this field. We did not compute quality scores. Instead, the full-text charting of the anchor set records which design features each study reports, and Section 4 discusses evidential weight qualitatively. Before any cross-study statement, findings were classified as directly comparable (same construct, measure and model), conditionally comparable (same construct but different measure or model) or non-comparable. No effect sizes were pooled and no leaderboards were constructed. Apparent disagreements were typed as a genuine contradiction, context-dependent, a metric artefact, a methodological artefact, or insufficient evidence (Table 7).

### 3.8 Reporting audit of the anchor set

To test whether the reporting practices recommended in Section 5.4 are already followed, the full texts of the 37 anchor studies were coded against the twelve checklist items (Table 8) using a frozen codebook (Supplementary File S5). Each item has an applicability rule; for example, the probe items apply only to studies that use activation probes or steering. Each study received one of four codes per item: reported, partly reported, not reported, or not applicable, with a verbatim quotation or a location for every positive code. Four AI coding agents coded disjoint subsets. A fifth agent, working independently and blind to the primary codes, re-coded a random sample of ten studies (seed 20261009). Agreement between the two independent codings over the 120 double-coded cells was 93.3% (Cohen's κ = 0.90); agreement on applicability alone was κ = 0.98, and agreement on the code when both coders judged the item applicable was κ = 0.84 (85 cells). The primary codes are analysed. Adherence to an item is the share of applicable studies that report it, counting partial reporting as one half. Because the checklist was derived partly from these studies, the audit describes reporting practice; it is not a validation of the checklist.
